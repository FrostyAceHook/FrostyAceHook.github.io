## Overview

Slinky is a 5 kN additively-manufactured, regeneratively-cooled LOx/IPA liquid rocket engine in which every geometric and operating parameter is produced by a model ([BRUV](https://github.com/JackGardiner/bruv)) rather than by hand. All 3D files were generated by its geometry module, and no external CAD was used.

Slinky was developed by a three-person capstone team over an accelerated 7-month period, DMLM printed in CuCrZr in partnership with [CSIRO Lab22](https://research.csiro.au/metals/add-manufacturing/aus-innovation/), and fired five times at [Race2Space](https://race2space.org.uk/) 2026, where it **won Best LOx Bi-Prop**.

My scope was the analysis and programming: geometry generation code, regenerative cooling and engine performance simulation, structural analysis, Bazarov co-axial swirl injector sizing, and data analysis for both the injector characterisation campaign and the hotfires.

## BRUV

All of the below lives in [BRUV](https://github.com/JackGardiner/bruv) (Bipropellant, Regeneratively-cooled, Unprecedentedly-optimised, V-kN engine maker), a C/C#/Python program where the geometry module is built on the [PicoGK](https://picogk.org/) kernel and no external CAD is used. The performance, cooling, and structural models run in sequence, and their combined outputs feed the geometry generation directly, so a change to a performance target propagates through to print-ready geometry.

<img src="/assets/projects/slinky/generation.gif" alt="BRUV generation gif">


### Simulation and optimisation layers

The sim module of BRUV is split into a core layer and an optimisation layer that wraps it. The core layer evaluates a fully specified engine. Some of that specification is reduced to a handful of parameters, with the chamber contour set by a Rao nozzle at variable truncation, while other inputs such as mass flow rates are given directly.

The optimisation layer takes a single input (though is structured to easily facilitate more), target thrust, and runs an optimisation algorithm over the design variables to a local minimum of the cost function. It returns a complete engine, including chamber contour, channel geometry, and propellant inlet pressures and flow rates. The cost function rewards hitting target thrust, higher Isp, and a higher minimum safety factor, with smaller terms for other goals such as lower IPA inlet pressure and thinner walls, plus penalties on physically impossible systems and on features below the printable minimum.

I wrote an implementation of Powell's method for the optimisation algorithm over the design variables. This was chosen because the cost function carries hard feasibility penalties and clamped intermediate quantities, so it is not smooth everywhere and finite-difference gradients would be unreliable near those boundaries. A derivative-free conjugate-direction search tolerates that, at the cost of more function evaluations.

The variables it searches are LOx and IPA flow rates, wall thicknesses, helix angles, and channel dimensions. Wall and channel parameters are defined point-wise along the contour, which on its own would give a search space of several hundred dimensions. They are instead constrained to interpolate between three control stations at the chamber, throat and exit, which keeps the problem manageable on a standard computer in minutes while still allowing the variable geometry to gain most of its advantage.


### Engine performance

The simulation layer evaluates a given engine across the chamber contour using NASA CEA for combustion properties, taken at equilibrium and cached as lookups over sub- and supersonic area ratios. The version that generated the print files handled real-world losses coarsely. A single 90% efficiency factor was applied to thrust along with a small divergence loss, which implicitly assumed C\* efficiency of 100% and left combustion inefficiency unrepresented. The 90% came from other teams' published 2025 Race2Space data. Hotfire data showed a true efficiency of 82.5% C\* and 92% Cf, a combined 75.9% against the 90% assumed. A refined model developed after print properly split the efficiencies across C* and Cf. Fitting both to one hotfire is a calibration rather than a check, so it is validated by holding those efficiencies fixed and simulating the other hotfires, covered in the results section.

### Cooling

The engine thermal state is solved as a quasi-1D marching problem along the chamber contour, discretised into 500 stations and stepped from the nozzle exit to the injector face, counter-flow against the gas path. Each station solves a full gas-to-coolant thermal stack, and the converged solution is used as the initial guess for the next station, which keeps the iteration count at each station low.

**Gas side:** Combustion properties are taken from the performance pass and are locally accurate at each station. Radiation is added as a gray gas at an emissivity of 0.15. Adiabatic wall temperature uses a turbulent recovery factor: $\ T_{aw} = T_g\left(1 + {\Pr}^{1/3}\,\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)$. Heat transfer coefficient comes from Bartz, with two refinements over the textbook form: properties are evaluated at the Eckert reference temperature rather than at freestream conditions, which required inverting the CEA table to find the area ratio corresponding to that reference temperature, and the throat curvature term uses the upstream radius (1.5 times the throat radius):

<div>
$$h_g = \frac{0.026}{D_t^{0.2}}
\left(\frac{\mu^{0.2}c_p}{\Pr^{0.6}}\right)_{\!\text{ref}}
\left(\frac{\dot m}{A_t}\right)^{0.8}
\left(\frac{D_t}{R_{curv}}\right)^{0.1}
\left(\frac{A_t}{A}\right)^{0.9}\sigma,
\qquad R_{curv} = 1.5\,R_t$$

$$\sigma = \left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{T_w}{T_0}
\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right]^{0.2\omega-0.8}
\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{-0.2\omega},
\qquad \omega = 0.6$$

$$T_{ref} = 0.5\,T_w + 0.28\,T_0 + 0.22\,T_{aw}$$
</div>

**Film cooling:** The IPA film cooling (injected at many discrete holes around the perimeter of the injector face but approximated as a continuous film) is modelled with an entrainment formulation rather than a fixed effectiveness. The film cooling fraction is a prescribed input, set to 15% here. To handle film travel distance correctly, its contour arc length is precomputed by polyline integration rather than approximated by axial position. An enthalpy balance between the entrained coolant and the core flow sets a protected gas temperature, accounting for liquid heating, vaporisation and vapour-phase heat capacity, which yields a local effectiveness. That effectiveness is then blended along the chamber as a function of arc length from the injector face, chamber radius and the ratio of core to film thermal capacity, so protection decays downstream as the liquid film is consumed. The result reduces the gas temperature seen by the wall, which then feeds Bartz on the next iteration.

<div>
$$H = \frac{\dot m_{fc}}{\dot m_{fc}+\dot m_g}\,\eta_c\,\frac{2\,(V_g/V_d)}{f}
\left(1 + \left(\frac{V_g}{V_d} - 1\right)^{c_{p,v}/c_{p,g}}\right)$$

$$T_{pr} = \frac{c_{p,v}T_{aw} + H\left(c_{p,l}T_{film} - h_{vap}\right)}
{c_{p,v} + H\,c_{p,l}},
\qquad
\eta_0 = \frac{T_{aw}-T_{pr}}{T_{aw}-T_{film}}$$

$$\eta = \eta_0 + \frac{1-\eta_0}{1 + A\,B\,\ell/2R_{cc}},
\qquad B = \frac{\dot m_g c_{p,g}}{\dot m_{fc}c_{p,l}},
\qquad T_{gw} = T_{aw} + \eta\left(T_{film}-T_{aw}\right)$$
</div>

with $\eta_c = 0.25$, $f = 0.035$, $V_g/V_d = 1.2$, $A = 0.37$ and
$T_{film} = 450$ K taken from as literature defaults, unvalidated against this engine.

**Coolant side:** IPA properties come from Python Thermo (cached in lookup tables) as a function of local temperature and pressure. Channel flow area and hydraulic diameter are taken from the generated channel cross-section, approximated as rectangular. Friction factor uses Colebrook with an as-printed roughness input of 135 μm (from Nikon CuCrZr datasheet), and Nusselt uses Dittus-Boelter.

<div>
$$D_h = \frac{2\psi t}{\psi + t}, \quad
G = \frac{\dot m_c}{A_c}, \quad
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{G D_h}{\mu}, \quad
\mathrm{Nu} = 0.023\,\mathrm{Re}^{0.8}{\Pr}^{0.4}, \quad
h_c = \frac{\mathrm{Nu}\,k}{D_h}$$
</div>

The channel webs are treated as fins, with an efficiency from the standard tanh solution, and the coolant-side coefficient is scaled by the resulting area weighting. Coolant enthalpy rise is carried station to station from the local flux over the frustum contact area, with pressure drop from Darcy-Weisbach.

**Wall stack:** Conduction is solved as cylindrical resistances in series: a PDMS deposition layer on the gas side, the CuCrZr inner wall, then the fin-corrected convective resistance to the coolant. The 2% PDMS additive is represented as an insulating deposit of thickness 30 μm and conductivity 1.3 W/m/K, which is why it appears as a thermal design variable and not only as a performance penalty. The station converges by fixed-point iteration on the three interface temperatures to a tolerance of 0.05 K.

**Feasibility:** The solver also returns whether the system is physically realisable. Coolant state leaving the bounds of the property tables, or channel pressure falling below zero, marks the design infeasible and rejects it before it reaches geometry generation. This is what lets the framework sweep design space without producing engines that cannot physically exist. Outputs are a wall temperature profile along the chamber, coolant outlet temperature and channel pressure drop, all of which were instrumented on the stand.

**Known limitations:** The model is steady-state only, so startup and shutdown transients are not captured, and modelling them was out of scope on this schedule. It also carries no axial conduction through the wall or the coolant, and the exhaust gas follows the CEA expansion without accounting for the enthalpy extracted at the wall. Neither simplification should matter much here, since axial temperature gradients are small relative to radial, and the heat pulled into the wall is negligible (on advice from more experienced teams rather than verified against data).

The coolant-side correlation is the more consequential gap. Dittus-Boelter assumes a smooth straight tube, so the model doesn't incorporate the curvature of the channels or the roughness of the as-printed surface, and although roughness enters the friction factor through Colebrook, it doesn't impact the Nusselt number. The feasibility check is similarly rough: the solver decides whether a system is possible by bounds checking rather than by any real analysis of convergence or physical reasoning about the fluid state, and a station that hits its iteration cap without converging is passed through rather than flagged, so a numerically awkward design and a genuinely impossible one are not always told apart.

There are two immediate improvements. Firstly replacing Dittus-Boelter with a Gnielinski or Sieder-Tate base, adding a Dean number correction for the channel curvature, and applying roughness augmentation to the Nusselt number instead of to friction alone. Secondly distinguishing physical infeasibility from numerical divergence or coolant state out-of-bounds, which would help the optimisation layer make better use of the full input space.

### Structural

The chamber structure is checked against the converged thermal output, with wall temperatures and coolant pressure interpolated from the thermal stations onto the stress stations, so calculations are driven by the same gas-side and coolant-side models rather than by an assumed temperature. Two load cases were run:

**Startup:** Before ignition the cooling channels are at full pressure with nothing in the chamber to push back, so the inner wall carries the entire coolant pressure across its span. It is treated as a fixed-fixed plate over the channel width, checked against room temperature yield since the wall is still cold.

<div>
$$\sigma = \frac{P_c}{2}\left(\frac{w_{ch}}{t_{iw}}\right)^{\!2},
\qquad \mathrm{SF} = \frac{\sigma_y(293\ \mathrm{K})}{\sigma}$$
</div>

**Firing:** At steady state, chamber pressure relieves most of the coolant load, so inner wall bending uses the pressure difference and becomes a minor term. Hoop stress is assembled from the pressure hoop over an effective thickness that smears web material across the channel span, a thermal component from the through-wall gradient, and the residual inner wall bending. Hoop and meridional combine into a von Mises stress checked against yield for a final per-station safety factor.

<div>
$$t_{\text{eff}} = t_{iw} + t_{ow} + \frac{w_{web}\,t_{ch}}{w_{web}+w_{ch}}$$

$$\sigma_\theta =
\underbrace{\frac{P_g r}{t_{\text{eff}}}}_{\text{pressure}} +
\underbrace{\frac{E\alpha\,\Delta T}{2(1-\nu)}}_{\text{thermal}} +
\underbrace{\frac{P_c - P_g}{2}\left(\frac{w_{ch}}{t_{iw}}\right)^{\!2}}_{\text{bending}},
\qquad \sigma_m = E\alpha\,\Delta T$$

$$\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma_\theta^2 - \sigma_\theta\sigma_m + \sigma_m^2},
\qquad \mathrm{SF} = \frac{\sigma_y(T_{wg})}{\sigma_{vm}}$$
</div>

Material properties are evaluated at the local hot wall temperature rather than at room temperature, which is very impactful for CuCrZr. Yield strength falls from 174 MPa at 293 K to 75 MPa at the ~550 K peak temperature predicted (data provided by CSIRO Lab22 materials team).

Wall thickness was never sized explicitly. Minimum safety factor is one term in the optimiser's cost function, weighed against wall thickness and the performance terms, so the structural model runs inside the design loop rather than as a check afterwards, and the geometry it converged on carries ~2.6 at firing against ~6.0 at startup. Firing governs by a wide margin, and the optimiser likely found a local minimum where a thicker inner wall lowers pressure hoop but raises the wall temperature and thermal stress, so safety factor peaks where the two trade off.

Both parts (injector and chamber) were proof tested to 70 bar, with no permanent deformation. The proof pressure was set by Race2Space, which caps inlet pressure at whatever the part has been proofed to, so proofing high gave ample feed system headroom. The startup case analysis provided sufficient confidence that the chamber could handle this proof, since it also applied a cold coolant-side load.

**Known limitations:** The analysis is static and handles no stress concentrations at corners. Creep and thermal ratcheting are not modelled, due to time constraints rather than an actual choice (though with only ~31 seconds of cumulative run time it wouldn't have impacted our results). Thin wall hoop is assumed throughout. Additionally, hoop thermal stress uses the constrained biaxial form while meridional currently uses the uniaxial form, which is conservative by a factor of 2(1-ν) for a wall that is in fact restrained in both directions. It's a known inconsistency rather than an actual choice, causing a change of roughly 10% in the reported safety factor.

The IPA inlet manifold, injector stresses, fasteners, and seals are not modelled or optimised by the sim module, and were instead hand-sized and verified by Jack Gardiner.

### Geometry generation

The simulation module above hands a concise parameterisation of the engine to the geometry module, which emits a print-ready STL. Additive manufacturing constraints are enforced by the construction logic rather than manually checked afterwards. These include limits such as minimum feature size, depowdering clearance, build angle limits, and overhang rules.

Every threaded hole is generated as an analytically determined shape: circular for vertical holes, diamond for horizontal and skewed diamonds in between, so that they print without support and can be faced and tapped to size in post-machining. Internal channels are routed into breakouts that give air-gun access during depowdering, and machining stock is left on the mating face and all sealing surfaces. Alongside the STL the module outputs in-build screenshots and structured text reports covering every important feature, which is what our machinist and team worked from during post-processing.

Generation time is set entirely by the requested output resolution. Production files were built at a PicoGK voxel size of 0.09 mm, chosen by inspecting exported STLs and taking the coarsest voxel that still resolved the smallest features cleanly, particularly the injector elements and the cooling channel fillets. A chamber run at that resolution takes around 16 hours on my 2017 normal-guy laptop and uses around 20 GB of memory. That runtime is the result of extensive profiling and optimising, with voxel offset operations and voxel to mesh conversions accounting for over 95% of it. The resulting meshes are larger than 5 GB, large enough that most programs (e.g. Blender, MeshLab) will not open them and will fail to operate on them, which is what prompted me to write [deci](/projects/deci) ([source](https://github.com/FrostyAceHook/deci)) over a few weekends.


## Injector sizing and validation

### Bazarov swirl injector

The 12-element internal-mixing bi-centrifugal swirl injector is sized by an implementation of Bazarov swirl theory written into BRUV. Given target mass flow, pressure drop and spray cone angle per element, it solves the geometric characteristic parameter for each swirler and returns tangential inlet dimensions, vortex chamber and nozzle diameters, filling coefficient and discharge coefficient for the inner and outer stages.

Element count was chosen to ensure manufacturability. More elements give better mixing, but shrink every feature, so the count was pushed up until the smallest passages reached the 0.7 mm printable minimum and a clear ring placement pattern was possible.

Stiffness was fixed at 20% of chamber pressure. The commonly cited floor for backflow margin and combustion stability is 15%, and the extra 5% cover the ways a swirl element can have higher than predicted flow. For example, as-printed roughness in the tangential passages reduces tangential velocity, which weakens the vortex and thickens the liquid film in the nozzle. A thicker film means a smaller air core, a higher discharge coefficient, and more flow for a given pressure drop.

### Characterisation campaign

The theory above predicts three things per element: mass flow against pressure drop, discharge coefficient, and spray cone angle. A dedicated flow rig was built over a few days early in the design phase to measure all three on physical samples.

The rig supplies two independent water channels at up to 30 bar and 0.2 kg/s, with a high-speed DAQ and an integrated DSLR lightbox for spray imaging, all controlled remotely through a custom GUI (pinGUI). Over 30 sample designs were tested across SLA resin and DMLM CuCrZr, covering a wide range of geometries and operating points.

Resin allowed fast iteration at low cost, and two batches of copper samples established a transfer correlation between the two materials, a consistent K-factor offset of roughly 21% more flow on copper samples. That let resin drive the design sweep while copper allowed calibration for the real injector. Against the Bazarov prediction, measured discharge coefficients came in 40% higher in stage 1 (LOx) and 27% lower in stage 2 (IPA). Results were consistent and repeatable across the sweep.

The assembled injector was flow tested the same way before shipping, using the aluminium proof plate to isolate each element ring in turn. Both stages matched their intended per-element flow at the design pressure drop, so the element passages were confirmed as-built against the sample campaign. This later turned out to be the measurement that made the hotfire injector data interpretable.

## Manufacturing

Slinky was printed in two parts, chamber and injector, split at the injector face plane, in DMLM CuCrZr with a 60 μm layer height. Each part was then depowdered, heat treated, sand-blasted, and machined: facing, threading, and a height reduction on the injector element inner posts. Then each part was deswarfed using tweezers, high-pressure water and N2 flow to 40 bar, and guitar wire agitation.

Both parts were then proof tested to 70 bar by hand pump. Sealing was provided by a custom CNC-machined aluminium plate mating to the injector face, drilled with two plugged ports (one per element ring) allowing each injector element to be flow tested individually against the sample characterisation.

Citric acid (20%) stripped the machining oxides, then the engine and fittings were oxygen cleaned with ethanol, sonicated, DI rinsed, and oven dried overnight. After transport to the UK it was assembled and mounted to a welded 12 mm Grade 250 steel thrust mount, ready to fire.

### Model correlation

The Race2Space test stand ran a mass flow controller which varies inlet pressure to hold the requested flow rates. Propellant flows were therefore at their design values throughout, which means every pressure discrepancy below is a resistance or performance finding rather than an artefact of flowing off-design. Values are from hotfire 2, the steady-state design point.

| Quantity | Predicted | Measured | Error |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Thrust | 5 kN | 4.2 kN | -16% |
| Chamber pressure | 35 bar | 29.5 bar | -16% |
| Injector DP, LOx | 7 bar | 8.9 bar | +27% |
| Injector DP, IPA | 7 bar | 4.6 bar | -34% |
| Channel pressure drop | 9.1 bar | 14 bar | +54% |
| Coolant temperature rise | 35 °C | 26.6 °C | -24% |

The coolant temperature rise is taken between the first and last instrumented stations rather than across the full contour, so predicted and measured cover the same length of channel. Instrument uncertainty was not characterised, so these are raw differences and the smaller discrepancies should not be taken as basis for findings.

**Cooling:** Coolant temperature was measured at six stations along the channel. Two effects are clear from this.

<div class="figrow">
  <img src="/assets/projects/slinky/temps-sim-vs-actual.png" alt="Simulated vs measured coolant temperature along the channel">
</div>

The measured profile sits above the simulation at all points, with the gap largest at the coolant inlet: roughly 14 °C at channel inlets, before any meaningful heat pickup has occurred. That is an incorrect boundary condition rather than a modelling error. The assumed inlet temperature of 25 °C did not account for the July 2026 UK heat wave, which was unfortunate for both the engine and us testers, and it left IPA sitting warm in the tank and feed lines and entering the channels nearer to 40 °C. Manifold heat soak contributes as well, though at these flow rates and burn durations it doesn't account for much.

With the inlet corrected, the model tracks the data closely from the exit plane through the throat and into the first part of the convergent section. That is the high-flux region and the hardest part of the contour to predict, so Bartz at the Eckert reference temperature, the coolant-side correlation, and the fin treatment are all behaving well there.

The two diverge more significantly through the chamber. Measured coolant runs progressively cooler than predicted from around 125 mm forward, reaching roughly 10 °C below the model at the injector face. The discrepancy is confined to the chamber and grows toward the injector, which rules out the terms that would bias the whole contour, such as a Bartz coefficient error or an over-predicted combustion temperature.

More likely is the film cooling model, which blends effectiveness along arc length from the injector face and decays it downstream as the film is consumed, so an error in that decay law compounds toward the injector and vanishes by the throat. Real film protection therefore appears better than modelled, most likely in how long the liquid film survives rather than in its initial effectiveness.

That is one of two readings, though, and the coolant temperature data alone cannot separate them. The coolant picked up about 76% of the predicted heat over the instrumented span. Either the gas transferred less heat into the wall than the model thought, or the wall transferred less heat into the coolant than the model thought. The two have opposite meanings where the first drops wall temperature and the second raises it. The larger-than-expected channel pressure drop result suggests the first cause, since every mechanism that would explain the extra pressure drop also raises the coolant-side coefficient rather than lowering it. The one exception is the channel cross-section itself, which is discussed there.

**Channel pressure drop:** a 54% higher pressure was required to achieve the intended channel mass flow rate than the model predicted, indicating the real channels were more resistive than the model believed.

Darcy-Weisbach scales pressure drop with roughly the fifth power of channel linear dimension at fixed mass flow, once the area and hydraulic diameter terms are combined. A reduction of about 8% in channel width and depth accounts for the whole discrepancy on its own. That is the size of powder adhesion on down-facing surfaces in DMLM, and it is the explanation I would put first. Two others point the same way and cannot be separated from it with the data I have: as-printed roughness above the 135 μm datasheet figure used in Colebrook, and curvature loss in the helical circuit, which a straight-tube friction factor does not represent at all. No flow characterisation of the as-printed channels was done, so it's unclear which of the three is the true cause.

The channel cross-section is also subject to a simplification compared to the true geometry. The model treats the channel as a rectangle (1.05 x 1.6 mm at the throat), while the printed channel carries 0.2 mm corner fillets. That removes about 2% of the flow area. It does not explain the pressure drop, because the fillets also reduce the wetted perimeter and raise the hydraulic diameter by about 5%, and the two effects very nearly cancel in Darcy-Weisbach. Where the fillets have a greater impact is heat transfer. They change the base of the webbing (modelled as a fin), and change the area weighting used to correct the coolant-side coefficient. So the fillets are the most plausible route to the coolant-side branch of the heat load question above, and the pressure drop is still best explained by adhesion, roughness and curvature.

**Injector pressure drop:** both stages were moderately inaccurate, in opposite directions, compared to the characterisation campaign that was consistent and repeatable across more than 30 samples. They are not equally interpretable, because the two circuits were not instrumented the same way.

The LOx side has a clean measurement on the manifold which only feeds the injector elements. The DP was 27% higher than expected, which means the discharge coefficient was about 11% lower than expected. A water flow test cannot reproduce cavitation or two-phase flow, and any two-phase flow within the injector element would reduce flow rate (or with a mass flow controller, raise pressure drop). This is an unconfirmed theory, because no LOx temperature was measured at the injector and so we cannot check the phase. The objective of the characterisation campaign for LOx was to simply support a decent margin rather than an accurate prediction, which was the right call and allowed this acceptable DP despite different flow and fluid conditions.

The IPA side is difficult to attribute a cause from this measurement. The PT tapped the manifold, which feeds both the injector elements and the film cooling holes as two parallel paths. The 4.6 bar therefore applies to the combined effective flow area of both, not the elements alone. At fixed total flow the measured drop implies a total effective CdA about 23% larger than predicted. Where that excess sits is unknown. If it is in the element passages, the characterisation campaign correlation did not transfer. If it is in the film holes, the film is drawing more than its designed 15% share and the chamber sees more film protection than modelled.

I lean toward the film holes, because that also causes the chamber coolant to run cooler than predicted on all five hotfires, as was measured. It is directly testable with hardware that already exists. The aluminium sealing plate built for proof testing flows each element ring on its own through its plugged ports, so measuring element CdA on the bench and differencing it against the manifold ΔP at known total flow settles the split. That is the first thing I would do with the engine on a bench again.

### Performance

The engine delivered 4.19 kN at the design point with a chamber pressure of 29.5 bar. The model that generated the print files was targeting a 5 kN thrust and 35 bar chamber pressure, and sized the engine assuming an essentially flat 10% thrust loss (also included a tiny divergent exhaust loss term) and assumed no chamber pressure loss. Hotfire data (#2, design point) gave efficiencies of 82.5% C\* and 92% Cf, combining to a 75.9% thrust efficiency against the 90% assumed. Despite the 17.5% chamber pressure drop, the exhaust was close to ideally expanded with no separation.

The 17.5% C\* deficit can be attributed to a few major reasons. First is film cooling. The core propellant flow was 2.16 kg/s, with 0.16 kg/s of IPA film. C\* is referenced to total flow, so assuming the film released no energy provides a maximum C\* reduction of (0.16/2.32) 6.9%. That is an upper bound, since some of the film will combust. Secondly, the 2% PDMS additive is inert and provides no additional energy. Its flow rate was 0.021 kg/s, under 1% of total flow, and so is worst case another 1% C\* deficit. The bulk of the remaining ~10% of efficiency loss is due to incomplete combustion. It's likely that is driven in part by the short L\* of 0.8 m (1.0 to 1.3 m is typical for LOx/IPA). Another likely possibility is poor injector mixing, however we have no current way of quantifying or confirming this.

The sim module was updated after print files were built to properly incorporate losses into C\* and Cf efficiencies instead of lumping it into thrust. Fitting two efficiencies to fire 2 is a calibration, not a check against the model (two free parameters will always perfectly match two measurements). To check that this is a valid modelling of engine efficiency, the calibration against hotfire #2 is tested against the other hotfires.

Hotfire #5 (±25% throttle) is the hardest case, since it spans 3.1 kN to 5.4 kN. Thrust tracks within a couple of percent across the whole sweep. Chamber pressure is slightly worse, at a 6% maximum error, and the error has a clear bias: at low flow the model predicts too much chamber pressure, at high flow too little. Real C\* efficiency therefore rises with chamber pressure. Losses that are large at low pressure shrink at high pressure, which is what you would expect from atomisation and reaction rate, and a fixed efficiency cannot capture it.

### Stability & Durability

The chamber and injector came out of five fires and 31 seconds of run time in near original condition. No soot deposition anywhere on the injector face, no tarnishing, and no erosion on any injector element. Converging section of chamber into throat and nozzle has significant PDMS and soot deposit, but that is essentially unavoidable with hydrocarbon propellants.

The one exception is the ignitor port. Between fires 1 and 3 it eroded at the inner edge, thinning the material there until the erosion stopped on its own. I believe the erosion occurred until the remaining thickness became thin enough for the manifold fluid to cool it, so the port found a stable geometry and held it for the remaining hotfires. It was the only feature on either part that changed across the tests, and it did not affect any of the five ignitions, but it was not designed for and it is not something I would leave alone on a longer duration engine.

The injector was sized to 20% stiffness on both stages against a 35 bar chamber. Measured against the delivered 29.5 bar chamber, the actual values were 30% on LOx and 16% on IPA. Stiffness scales roughly with throttle setting for fixed geometry, which meant the IPA stiffness was ~12% at the bottom of the ±25% sweep.

The engine ran at all these points without incident. Chamber pressure was steady through both throttle sweeps and the OFR sweep, the plume was steady on all five fires, and teardown found no localised heating which instability typically leaves behind. No spectral analysis of the chamber pressure trace has been performed yet, but there was no visible instability observed and chug would be clear in the data if it were present.

### Future changes

**Model:** The coolant side had the largest discrepancies between modelled and measured. I would use the real filleted cross-section rather than the rectangle, which fixes both the flow area and the fin root, add a Dean number correction for the helical curvature, and apply roughness effects to the Nusselt number rather than to only friction. Next would be the film cooling model, recalibrated against this coolant profile and ideally a dedicated film rig. The efficiency treatment should carry a pressure dependence instead of two fixed numbers, since Hotfire #5 shows that dependence directly. Finally, the feasibility check should tell numerical divergence apart from genuine physical impossibility, which currently it does not, so the optimiser can use more of its input space without being handed false rejections.

**Engine:** The three contributing C\* deficit factors can all be addressed and reduced. A longer L\* increases the residence time, a film cooling fraction well below 15% allows more combustion at a more efficient OFR, and removing or reducing the PDMS removes inert mass. All three increase C\* and all three increase the heat load, which is a trade that the data indicates is completely possible while still maintaining a working engine. The ignitor port should also be machined with a chamfer or similar, to start at a stable thickness rather than eroding down to it.

**Instrumentation:** Many of the open questions on this page are because of sensor limitations rather than because of model inaccuracies. A temperature probe on the LOx side at the injector helps confirm the existence two-phase flow. Wall thermocouples would separate the gas-side and coolant-side branches of the heat load directly. More thermocouples in the channels and measuring more than one channel allows a much better reading of the temperature contour. These changes are essentially free from a design perspective, and each would allow a much more confident analysis.
