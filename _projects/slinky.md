## Overview

Slinky is a 5 kN additively-manufactured, regeneratively-cooled LOx/IPA liquid rocket engine in which every geometric and operating parameter is produced by a model ([BRUV](https://github.com/JackGardiner/bruv)) rather than by hand. All 3D files were generated by its geometry module, and no external CAD was used.

Slinky was developed by a three-person capstone team over an accelerated 7-month period, DMLM printed in CuCrZr in partnership with [CSIRO Lab22](https://www.csiro.au/en/research/production/materials/lab22), and fired five times at [Race2Space](https://race2space.org.uk/) 2026, where it **won Best LOx Bi-Prop**.

My scope was the analysis and programming: geometry generation code, regenerative cooling and engine performance simulation, structural analysis, Bazarov co-axial swirl injector sizing, and data analysis for both the injector characterisation campaign and the hotfires.

## BRUV

Everything below is part of [BRUV](https://github.com/JackGardiner/bruv) (Bipropellant, Regeneratively-cooled, Unprecedentedly-optimised, V-kN engine maker), a C/C#/Python program where the geometry module is built on the [PicoGK](https://picogk.org/) kernel and no external CAD is used. The performance, cooling, and structural models run in sequence, and their combined outputs feed the geometry generation directly, so a change to a performance target carries through to print-ready geometry.

<img src="/assets/projects/slinky/generation.gif" alt="BRUV generation gif">


### Simulation and optimisation layers

The sim module of BRUV is split into a core layer and an optimisation layer that wraps it. The core layer evaluates a fully specified engine. Some of that specification is reduced to a handful of parameters, with the chamber contour set by a Rao nozzle at variable truncation, while other inputs such as mass flow rates are given directly.

The optimisation layer takes one input, target thrust, although its structure allows more. It runs an optimisation algorithm over the design variables to find a local minimum of the cost function, and returns a complete engine including chamber contour, channel geometry, and propellant inlet pressures and flow rates.

The cost function rewards hitting target thrust, higher Isp, and a higher minimum safety factor, with smaller terms for other goals such as lower IPA inlet pressure and thinner walls, plus penalties on physically impossible systems and on features below the printable minimum.

I wrote an implementation of Powell's method as the optimisation algorithm. This was chosen because the cost function carries hard feasibility penalties and clamped intermediate quantities, so it is not smooth everywhere, and finite-difference gradients would be unreliable near those boundaries. A derivative-free conjugate-direction search tolerates that, at the cost of more function evaluations.

The variables it searches are LOx and IPA flow rates, wall thicknesses, helix angles, and channel dimensions. The wall and channel parameters are defined point-wise along the contour, which alone would give a search space of several hundred dimensions. I constrained them instead to interpolate between three control stations at the chamber, throat, and exit, which keeps the search to a few minutes on a standard computer while still allowing the variable geometry to gain most of its advantage.


### Engine performance

The simulation layer evaluates an engine across the chamber contour. Combustion properties come from NASA CEA, taken at equilibrium and cached as lookups over sub- and supersonic area ratios. The program is able to take a specified chamber contour and calculate steady-state pressure or take a steady-state pressure target and calculate the chamber contour which would give that pressure.

The version that generated the print files handled real-world losses coarsely. It applied one 90% efficiency factor to thrust, plus a small divergence loss. That implied a C\* efficiency of 100%, and left chamber pressure losses out of the model. The 90% thrust efficiency came from other teams' published 2025 Race2Space data. Hotfire data then showed 82.5% C\* efficiency and 92% Cf efficiency, which combine to 75.9% thrust efficiency.

A refined model, developed after the print, splits the efficiency properly across C\* and Cf. Fitting both numbers to one hotfire is a calibration rather than a check, so I have validated it by holding those efficiencies fixed and simulating the other hotfires. The results section covers that.

### Cooling

The model solves the engine thermal state as a quasi-1D marching problem along the chamber contour. It discretises the contour into 500 stations, and steps counter-flow against the gas path from the nozzle exit to the injector face. Each station solves a full gas-to-coolant thermal stack, and the converged solution becomes the initial guess for the next station, which keeps the iteration count low at each station.

**Gas side:** Combustion properties comr from the performance pass, and are locally accurate at each station. Radiation is added as a gray gas at an emissivity of 0.15. Adiabatic wall temperature uses a turbulent recovery factor: $\ T_{aw} = T_g\left(1 + {\Pr}^{1/3}\,\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)$. The heat transfer coefficient comes from Bartz, with two changes to the textbook form. First, properties are evaluated at the Eckert reference temperature rather than at freestream conditions, which required inverting the CEA table to find the area ratio for that reference temperature. Second, the throat curvature term uses the upstream radius (1.5 times the throat radius).

<div>
$$h_g = \frac{0.026}{D_t^{0.2}}
\left(\frac{\mu^{0.2}c_p}{\Pr^{0.6}}\right)_{\!\text{ref}}
\left(\frac{\dot m}{A_t}\right)^{0.8}
\left(\frac{D_t}{R_{curv}}\right)^{0.1}
\left(\frac{A_t}{A}\right)^{0.9}\sigma,
\qquad R_{curv} = 1.5\,R_t$$

$$\sigma = \left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{T_w}{T_0}
\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right]^{0.2\omega-0.8}
\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{-0.2\omega},
\qquad \omega = 0.6$$

$$T_{ref} = 0.5\,T_w + 0.28\,T_0 + 0.22\,T_{aw}$$
</div>

**Film cooling:** The IPA film cooling is injected through many discrete holes around the perimeter of the injector face, but the model approximates it as a continuous film. It's modelled with an entrainment formulation rather than a fixed effectiveness. The film cooling fraction is an input, set to 15% here. Film travel distance is the contour arc length, precomputed by polyline integration rather than approximated by axial position.

An enthalpy balance between the entrained coolant and the core flow sets a protected gas temperature. It accounts for liquid heating, vaporisation, and vapour-phase heat capacity, and yields a local effectiveness. The model then blends that effectiveness along the chamber as a function of arc length from the injector face, chamber radius, and the ratio of core to film thermal capacity. Protection therefore decays downstream as the liquid film is consumed. The lower gas temperature at the wall then feeds Bartz on the next iteration.

<div>
$$H = \frac{\dot m_{fc}}{\dot m_{fc}+\dot m_g}\,\eta_c\,\frac{2\,(V_g/V_d)}{f}
\left(1 + \left(\frac{V_g}{V_d} - 1\right)^{c_{p,v}/c_{p,g}}\right)$$

$$T_{pr} = \frac{c_{p,v}T_{aw} + H\left(c_{p,l}T_{film} - h_{vap}\right)}
{c_{p,v} + H\,c_{p,l}},
\qquad
\eta_0 = \frac{T_{aw}-T_{pr}}{T_{aw}-T_{film}}$$

$$\eta = \eta_0 + \frac{1-\eta_0}{1 + A\,B\,\ell/2R_{cc}},
\qquad B = \frac{\dot m_g c_{p,g}}{\dot m_{fc}c_{p,l}},
\qquad T_{gw} = T_{aw} + \eta\left(T_{film}-T_{aw}\right)$$
</div>

The values $\eta_c = 0.25$, $f = 0.035$, $V_g/V_d = 1.2$, $A = 0.37$, and $T_{film} = 450$ K are literature defaults, and are not validated against this engine.

**Coolant side:** IPA properties come from Python Thermo as a function of local temperature and pressure, cached in lookup tables. Channel flow area and hydraulic diameter are taken from the generated channel cross-section, approximated as rectangular. The friction factor uses Colebrook, with an as-printed roughness of 135 μm from Nikon CuCrZr datasheet. The Nusselt number uses Dittus-Boelter.

<div>
$$D_h = \frac{2\psi t}{\psi + t}, \quad
G = \frac{\dot m_c}{A_c}, \quad
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{G D_h}{\mu}, \quad
\mathrm{Nu} = 0.023\,\mathrm{Re}^{0.8}{\Pr}^{0.4}, \quad
h_c = \frac{\mathrm{Nu}\,k}{D_h}$$
</div>

The model treats the channel webs as fins, with an efficiency from the standard tanh solution, and scales the coolant-side coefficient by the resulting area weighting. Coolant enthalpy rise is carried station to station from the local flux over the frustum contact area. Pressure drop comes from Darcy-Weisbach.

**Wall stack:** The model solves conduction as cylindrical resistances in series: a PDMS deposition layer on the gas side, the CuCrZr inner wall, then the fin-corrected convective resistance to the coolant. The 2% PDMS additive is represented as an insulating deposit of thickness 30 μm and conductivity 1.3 W/m/K. Each station converges by fixed-point iteration on the three interface temperatures, to a tolerance of 0.05 K.

**Feasibility:** The model also returns whether the system is physically realisable. If the coolant state leaves the bounds of the property tables, or channel pressure drops below zero, the model marks the design as infeasible and rejects it before reaching geometry generation. This is what lets the framework search the design space without producing engines that cannot physically exist. The outputs are a wall temperature profile along the chamber, the coolant outlet temperature, and the channel pressure drop. We measured all three at the hotfire.

**Known limitations:** The model is steady-state only, so it does not capture startup and shutdown transients. Modelling them was out of scope on this schedule. It also carries no axial conduction through the wall or the coolant, and the exhaust gas follows the CEA expansion without accounting for the enthalpy extracted at the wall. Neither simplification should matter much here, because axial temperature gradients are small relative to radial, and the heat absorbed by the wall is negligible (I've taken this on advice from more experienced teams rather than personally verifying it).

The coolant-side correlation is the larger gap. Dittus-Boelter assumes a smooth straight tube, so the model doesn't incorporate the curvature of the channels or the roughness of the as-printed surface, and although roughness enters the friction factor through Colebrook, it doesn't change the Nusselt number.

The feasibility check is similarly rough. The solver decides whether a system is possible by a bounds check rather than by any analysis of convergence or physical reasoning about the fluid state, and a station that reaches its iteration cap without converging is passed through rather than flagged. The solver therefore cannot always tell a numerically awkward design apart from a genuinely impossible one.

There are two immediate improvements. One is to replace Dittus-Boelter with a Gnielinski or Sieder-Tate base, add a Dean number correction for the channel curvature, and apply roughness augmentation to the Nusselt number instead of to friction alone. The other is to separate physical infeasibility from numerical divergence and from out-of-bounds states, which would let the optimisation layer make better use of the full input space.

### Structural

The chamber structure is checked against the converged thermal output. Wall temperatures and coolant pressure are interpolated from the thermal stations onto the stress stations. The same gas-side and coolant-side models therefore drive the stress calculation, rather than an assumed temperature. I ran two load cases:

**Startup:** Before ignition, the cooling channels are at full pressure with no chamber pressure as opposition, so the inner wall carries the entire coolant pressure across its span. The model treats it as a fixed-fixed plate over the channel width, and checks against room temperature yield, since the wall is still cold.

<div>
$$\sigma = \frac{P_c}{2}\left(\frac{w_{ch}}{t_{iw}}\right)^{\!2},
\qquad \mathrm{SF} = \frac{\sigma_y(293\ \mathrm{K})}{\sigma}$$
</div>

**Firing:** At steady state, chamber pressure relieves most of the coolant load. Inner wall bending then uses the pressure difference, and becomes a minor term. Hoop stress has three parts: the pressure hoop over an effective thickness that smears web material across the channel span, a thermal component from the through-wall gradient, and the residual inner wall bending. Hoop and meridional stress combine into a von Mises stress, checked against yield for an ultimate per-station safety factor.

<div>
$$t_{\text{eff}} = t_{iw} + t_{ow} + \frac{w_{web}\,t_{ch}}{w_{web}+w_{ch}}$$

$$\sigma_\theta =
\underbrace{\frac{P_g r}{t_{\text{eff}}}}_{\text{pressure}} +
\underbrace{\frac{E\alpha\,\Delta T}{2(1-\nu)}}_{\text{thermal}} +
\underbrace{\frac{P_c - P_g}{2}\left(\frac{w_{ch}}{t_{iw}}\right)^{\!2}}_{\text{bending}},
\qquad \sigma_m = E\alpha\,\Delta T$$

$$\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma_\theta^2 - \sigma_\theta\sigma_m + \sigma_m^2},
\qquad \mathrm{SF} = \frac{\sigma_y(T_{wg})}{\sigma_{vm}}$$
</div>

Material properties are evaluated at the local hot wall temperature, at room temperature. This matters a lot for CuCrZr, since its yield strength falls from 174 MPa at 293 K to 75 MPa at the predicted peak of ~550 K. The CSIRO Lab22 materials team supplied the data.

I never sized wall thicknesses explicitly. Minimum safety factor is one term in the cost function, weighed against wall thickness and the performance terms. Since the structural model runs inside the design loop rather than as a check afterwards, it allows the sim module to converge on geometry which carries a safety factor of ~2.6 at firing against ~6.0 at startup. Firing governs by a wide margin, and the optimiser probably found a local minimum where a thicker inner wall lowers the pressure hoop but raises wall temperature and thermal stress, so safety factor peaks where the two balance.

We proof tested both parts, the injector and the chamber, to 70 bar with no permanent deformation. Race2Space caps inlet pressure at the proof pressure of the part, so a high proof gave us plenty of feed pressure margin. The startup case gave enough confidence that the chamber would survive this proof, since it also applies a cold coolant-side load.

**Known limitations:** The analysis is static, and it handles no stress concentration at corners. It does not model creep or thermal ratcheting, due to time constraints rather than a decision, though at only ~31 seconds of cumulative run time neither would've have changed our results. Thin wall hoop is assumed throughout.

Hoop thermal stress also uses the constrained biaxial form while the meridional term uses the uniaxial form. For a wall that's restrained in both directions, that's conservative by a factor of 2(1-ν). It's a known inconsistency rather than a decision, causing a change of roughly 10% in the reported safety factor.

The IPA inlet manifold, injector stresses, fasteners, and seals are not modelled or optimised by the sim module, and were instead hand-sized and verified by Jack Gardiner.

### Geometry generation

The sim module passes a concise parameterisation of the engine to the geometry module, which outputs a print-ready STL. The construction logic enforces the additive manufacturing constraints, rather than a person checking them afterwards. Those constraints include minimum feature size, depowdering clearance, build angle limits, and overhang rules.

Every threaded hole is generated as an analytically determined shape: circular for vertical holes, diamond for horizontal, and skewed diamonds in between. They can therefore be printed without support, and post-machining can face and tap them to size. Internal channels route into breakouts that give air-gun access during depowdering, and machining stock is left on the mating face and on all sealing surfaces. Alongside the STL, the module outputs in-build screenshots and structured text reports covering every important feature, which is what our machinist and team worked from during post-processing.

Generation time is determined by the requested output resolution. We built the production files at a PicoGK voxel size of 0.09 mm. I chose that size by inspecting exported STLs and taking the coarsest voxel that still resolved the smallest features cleanly, mainly the injector elements and the cooling channel fillets. A chamber run at that resolution takes around 16 hours on my 2017 normal-guy laptop, and uses around 20 GB of memory. That runtime is already the result of extensive profiling and optimising, and voxel offset operations and voxel-to-mesh conversions account for over 95% of it.

The resulting meshes are larger than 5 GB, large enough that most programs (e.g. Blender, MeshLab) will not open them and will fail to operate on them, which is what prompted me to write [deci](/projects/deci) ([source](https://github.com/FrostyAceHook/deci)) over a few weekends.

### Bazarov swirl injector

The 12-element internal-mixing bi-centrifugal swirl injector is sized by an implementation of Bazarov swirl theory written into BRUV. It takes a target mass flow, pressure drop, and spray cone angle per element. It then solves the geometric characteristic parameter for each swirler, and from that the entire element's dimensions. For the inner and outer stages, it returns the tangential inlet dimensions, the vortex chamber and nozzle diameters, the filling coefficient, and the discharge coefficient for the inner and outer stages.

Element count was chosen to ensure manufacturability. More elements give better mixing, but they make every feature smaller. I therefore raised the count until the smallest passages reached the 0.7 mm printable minimum, and a clear ring placement pattern was possible.

I fixed injector stiffness at 20% of chamber pressure. The commonly cited floor for backflow margin and combustion stability is 15%, and the extra 5% cover the ways a swirl element can have higher than predicted flow. For example, as-printed roughness in the tangential passages lowers tangential velocity, which weakens the vortex and thickens the liquid film in the nozzle. A thicker film means a smaller air core, a higher discharge coefficient, and more flow at a given pressure drop.

### Characterisation campaign

The theory above predicts three things per element: mass flow against pressure drop, discharge coefficient, and spray cone angle. We built a dedicated flow rig over a few days early in the design phase to measure all three on physical samples.

The rig supplies two independent water channels at up to 30 bar and 0.2 kg/s. It carries a high-speed DAQ and an integrated DSLR lightbox for spray imaging, and a custom GUI (pinGUI) controls and monitors all of it remotely. We tested over 30 sample designs in SLA resin and DMLM CuCrZr, covering a wide range of geometries and operating points.

Resin allowed fast iteration at low cost, and two batches of copper samples established a transfer correlation between the two materials: a consistent K-factor offset of roughly 21% more flow on copper. Resin could therefore be used for the design sweep while the copper calibrated the real injector. Against the Bazarov prediction, measured discharge coefficients were 40% higher in stage 1 (LOx) and 27% lower in stage 2 (IPA). The results were consistent and repeatable across the sweep.

We flow tested the assembled injector the same way before hotfire, and used the aluminium proof plate to isolate each element ring in turn. Both stages matched their intended per-element flow at the design pressure drop, which confirmed the element passages as-built against the sample campaign.

## Manufacturing

Slinky was printed in two parts, the chamber and the injector, split at the injector face plane. Both are DMLM CuCrZr with a 60 μm layer height. Each part was then depowdered, heat treated, sand-blasted, and machined. Machining covered facing, threading, and a height reduction on the injector element inner posts. Each part was then deswarfed with tweezers, water and N2 flow up to 40 bar, and guitar wire agitation.

Both parts were then proof tested to 70 bar by hand pump. Sealing was provided by a custom CNC-machined aluminium plate mating to the injector face. It is drilled with two plugged ports, one per element ring, so each injector element can be flow tested individually against the sample characterisation.

A 20% citric acid bath was then used to remove any oxides, then the engine and fittings were oxygen cleaned with ethanol, sonicated, DI rinsed, and oven dried overnight. After transport to the UK, we assembled the engine and mounted it to a welded 12 mm Grade 250 steel thrust mount, ready to fire.

### Model correlation

The Race2Space test stand ran a mass flow controller, which varies inlet pressure to hold the requested flow rates. Propellant flows were therefore at their design values throughout, which means every pressure discrepancy below is a resistance or performance finding rather than an artefact of flowing off-design. The values are from hotfire 2, the steady-state design point.

| Quantity | Predicted | Measured | Error |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Thrust | 5 kN | 4.2 kN | -16% |
| Chamber pressure | 35 bar | 29.5 bar | -16% |
| Injector pressure drop, LOx | 7 bar | 8.9 bar | +27% |
| Injector pressure drop, IPA | 7 bar | 4.6 bar | -34% |
| Channel pressure drop | 9.1 bar | 14 bar | +54% |
| Coolant temperature rise | 35 °C | 26.6 °C | -24% |

The coolant temperature rise is taken between the first and last instrumented stations rather than across the full contour, so the predicted and measured cover the same length of channel. We did not characterise instrument uncertainty, so these are raw differences and the smaller discrepancies are not a basis for any finding.

**Cooling:** Coolant temperature was measured at six stations along the channel. Two effects are clear from this.

<div class="figrow">
  <img src="/assets/projects/slinky/temps-sim-vs-actual.png" alt="Simulated vs measured coolant temperature along the channel">
</div>

The measured profile sits above the simulation at every point. The gap is largest at the coolant inlet, roughly 14 °C, before the coolant absorbs any real heat from the chamber. That is an incorrect boundary condition rather than a modelling error. The assumed inlet temperature of 25 °C did not account for the July 2026 UK heat wave, which was unfortunate for both the engine and us testers. It left the IPA sitting warm in the tank and the feed lines, and entering the channels nearer to 40 °C. Manifold heat soak contributes as well, though at these flow rates and burn durations it does not account for much.

With the inlet corrected, the model tracks the data closely from the exit plane, through the throat, and into the first part of the convergent section. That's the high-flux region and the hardest part of the contour to predict. Therefore Bartz at the Eckert reference temperature, the coolant-side correlation, and the fin treatment all work well there.

The two diverge more through the chamber. From around 125 mm forward, the measured coolant runs progressively cooler than predicted, and reaches roughly 10 °C below the model at the injector face. The discrepancy is confined to the chamber and grows toward the injector, which rules out the terms that would bias the whole contour, such as a Bartz coefficient error or an over-predicted combustion temperature.

The film cooling model is the more likely cause. It blends effectiveness along arc length from the injector face, and decays it downstream as the film is consumed. An error in that decay law therefore compounds toward the injector and disappears by the throat. Real film protection therefore appears better than modelled, most likely in how long the liquid film survives rather than in its initial effectiveness.

That is one of two readings, though, and the coolant temperature data alone cannot separate them. The coolant absorbed about 76% of the predicted heat over the instrumented span. Either the gas transferred less heat into the wall than the model predicted, or the wall transferred less heat into the coolant than the model predicted. The two have opposite meanings, where the first lowers wall temperature and the second raises it. The larger-than-expected channel pressure drop points to the first, because every mechanism that would explain the extra pressure drop also raises the coolant-side coefficient rather than lowering it. The one exception is the channel cross-section itself, which I discuss below.

**Channel pressure drop:** The channels needed 54% more pressure than the model predicted to reach the intended mass flow rate. The real channels are therefore more resistive than the model expects.

Darcy-Weisbach scales pressure drop with roughly the fifth power of channel linear dimension at fixed mass flow, once the area and hydraulic diameter terms are combined. A reduction of about 8% in channel width and depth therefore accounts for the whole discrepancy on its own. That is on the order of the size of powder adhesion on down-facing surfaces in DMLM, and it is the explanation I would put first. Two other causes point the same way, and my data cannot separate them from it. The first is as-printed roughness above the 135 μm datasheet figure used in Colebrook. The second is curvature loss in the helical circuit, which a straight-tube friction factor does not represent at all. We did no flow characterisation of the as-printed channels, so it's unclear which of the three dominates.

The model also simplifies the channel cross-section. It treats the channel as a rectangle (1.05 x 1.6 mm at the throat), while the printed channel has 0.2 mm corner fillets. The fillets remove about 2% of the flow area. They do not explain the pressure drop, because they also reduce the wetted perimeter and raise the hydraulic diameter by about 5%, and the two effects essentially cancel in Darcy-Weisbach. The fillets matter more for heat transfer. They change the base of the webbing, which the model treats as a fin, and they change the area weighting that corrects the coolant-side coefficient. The fillets are therefore the most plausible route to the coolant-side branch of the heat load question above, and the pressure drop is still best explained by adhesion, roughness and curvature.

**Injector pressure drop:** Both stages missed the prediction moderately, in opposite directions, against a characterisation campaign that was consistent and repeatable across more than 30 samples. The two results are not equally interpretable, because we did not measure the two circuits in the same way.

The LOx side has a clean measurement on the manifold which only feeds the injector elements. The pressure drop was 27% higher than expected, so the discharge coefficient was about 13% lower than expected. A water flow test cannot reproduce cavitation or two-phase flow, and any two-phase flow inside an injector element would reduce the flow rate (or with a mass flow controller, raise pressure drop). This is an unconfirmed theory, because we measured no LOx temperature at the injector, so we cannot check the phase. LOx, the characterisation campaign only aimed to support a decent margin rather than an accurate prediction. That was the right call, and it is why this pressure drop is acceptable despite the different flow and fluid conditions.

The IPA side is harder to attribute a cause to from this measurement. The pressure transducer tapped the manifold, which feeds both the injector elements and the film cooling holes as two parallel paths. The 4.6 bar therefore applies to the combined effective flow area of both, not to the elements alone. At fixed total flow, the measured drop implies a total effective CdA about 23% larger than predicted. Where that excess sits is unknown. If it is in the element passages, the characterisation campaign correlation did not transfer. If it is in the film holes, the film is drawing more than its designed 15% share, and the chamber gets more film protection than modelled. I lean toward the film holes, because that also causes the chamber coolant to run cooler than predicted on all five hotfires, which is what we measured.

### Performance

The engine delivered 4.19 kN at the design point, with a chamber pressure of 29.5 bar. The model that generated the print files targeted a 5 kN thrust and 35 bar chamber pressure. It sized the engine on an essentially flat 10% thrust loss, plus a tiny divergent exhaust loss term, and no chamber pressure loss. Hotfire 2, the design point, gave efficiencies of 82.5% C\* and 92% Cf, which combine to a 75.9% thrust efficiency against the 90% assumed. Despite the 17.5% shortfall chamber pressure, the exhaust was close to ideally expanded, with no separation.

Three causes account for the 17.5% C\* deficit.

The first is film cooling. Core propellant flow was 2.16 kg/s, with 0.16 kg/s of IPA film. C\* is referenced to total flow, so a film that released no energy at all would reduce C\* by 6.9% (0.16/2.32). That is an upper bound, because some of the film will combust.

The second is the 2% PDMS additive, which is inert and adds no energy except its pressure. Its flow rate was 0.021 kg/s, under 1% of total flow, so it costs at worst another 1% of C\* reduction.

The third is incomplete combustion, which is likely significant enough to cover the remaining ~10%. The short L\* of 0.8 m probably drives part of it, since 1.0 to 1.3 m is typical for LOx/IPA. Poor injector mixing is the other likely cause, however we have no simple way to quantify or confirm this.

After the print files were built, I updated the sim module to carry losses as separate C\* and Cf efficiencies instead of assuming losses only affect thrust. Fitting two efficiencies to hotfire 2 is a calibration, not a check against the model, because two free parameters will always match two measurements exactly. To test whether this models engine efficiency correctly, I have appled the hotfire 2 calibration it to the other hotfires.

Hotfire 5 (±25% throttle) is the hardest case, since it spans 3.1 kN to 5.4 kN. Thrust tracks within a couple of percent across the whole sweep. Chamber pressure is slightly worse, at a 6% maximum error, and that error has a clear bias. At low flow the model predicts too much chamber pressure, and at high flow too little. Real C\* efficiency therefore rises with chamber pressure. Losses that are large at low pressure become smaller at high pressure, which is what you would expect from atomisation and reaction rate. A fixed efficiency cannot capture that.

### Stability & Durability

The chamber and the injector finished five fires and 31 seconds of run time in near original condition. There was no soot on the injector face, no tarnishing, and no erosion on any injector element. The converging section, the throat, and the nozzle had a significant PDMS and soot deposit, but that is essentially unavoidable with hydrocarbon propellants.

The one exception is the ignitor port. Between hotfires 1 and 3 it eroded at the inner edge, thinning the material there until the erosion stopped on its own. I believe it eroded until the remaining thickness became thin enough for the manifold fluid to cool it. Once at this thickness (at the end of hotfire 3), it did not change after subsequent fires, and so found a balance. It was the only feature on either part that changed across the tests, and it did not affect any of the five ignitions. It was still not designed for, and I would not leave it alone on a longer duration engine.

I sized the injector to 20% stiffness on both stages against a 35 bar chamber. Against the delivered 29.5 bar chamber, the actual values were 30% on LOx and 16% on IPA. The lowest stiffness occured at the lower end of the ±25% sweep, with an IPA stiffness of ~12%.

The engine ran at all these points without incident. Chamber pressure was steady through both throttle sweeps and the OFR sweep, and the plume was steady on all five fires. Teardown found none of the localised heating that instability typically leaves behind. I have not yet run a spectral analysis of the chamber pressure trace. We observed no instability, though, and chug would be clear in the data if it were present.

### Future changes

**Model:** The coolant side had the largest discrepancies between modelled and measured. I would make three changes there. First, use the real filleted cross-section rather than the rectangle, which corrects both the flow area and the fin root. Seond, add a Dean number correction for the helical curvature. Third, apply roughness effects to the Nusselt number rather than to only friction.

Next would be the film cooling model, recalibrated against this coolant profile and ideally against a dedicated film rig. The efficiency treatment should carry a pressure dependence instead of two fixed numbers, because hotfire 5 shows that dependence directly. Finally, the feasibility check should tell numerical divergence apart from genuine physical impossibility, which it currently does not. The optimiser could then use more of its input space without false rejections.

**Engine:** All three causes of the C\* deficit can be addressed and reduced. A longer L\* increases residence time. A film cooling fraction well below 15% allows more combustion at a more efficient OFR. Removing or reducing the PDMS removes inert mass. All three raise C\*, and all three raise the heat load. The data indicates that this trade is completely possible while maintaining an intact engine. The ignitor port should also be machined with a chamfer or similar, to start at a stable thickness rather than eroding down to it.

**Instrumentation:** Sensor limits, not model inaccuracies, cause many of the open questions on this page. A temperature probe at the injector on the LOx side would confirm whether two-phase flow exists. Wall thermocouples would separate the gas-side and coolant-side branches of the heat load directly. More thermocouples in the channels, and in more than one channel, would give a much better reading of the temperature contour. These changes cost almost nothing in the design, and each would allow a much more confident analysis.
